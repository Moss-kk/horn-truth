Egypt’s argument against Ethiopian dams is now almost a ritual. Addis Ababa, we are told, must give prior notification. It must not take a step on the Nile without Cairo’s leave. The language sounds like international law. The history does not.

If prior notification is the rule, the first question is simple. When did Egypt ask Ethiopia?

## A river divided without the country that feeds it

Most of the water that reaches Aswan does not fall on Egypt. Standard hydrological accounts say about 86 percent of that flow is born in Ethiopia: the Abbay (Blue Nile) alone about 59 percent, the Baro-Akobo (Sobat) about 14 percent, the Tekeze-Atbara about 13 percent. A state that contributes almost none of the water at the source nonetheless claimed the right to divide it.

In 1959 Egypt and Sudan signed the Agreement for the Full Utilization of the Nile Waters. They split 74 billion cubic metres a year between themselves: 55.5 billion to Egypt, 18.5 billion to Sudan. Ethiopia was not at the table. Ethiopia was not allocated a share. The treaty’s title said “full utilization,” as if the basin ended at the desert.

That was not prior notification. It was prior exclusion.

## Aswan, then Toshka

The Aswan High Dam was built between 1960 and 1970 on the strength of that closed deal. It is one of the largest water works of the twentieth century. It stores Nile floodwater for Egypt, generates power, and changed the river’s regime for everyone downstream of the sluices — and, in practice, for everyone upstream who would later be told that the stored “acquired rights” were already spoken for.

Ethiopia was not asked to approve Aswan. It was not invited to design the reservoir. It was expected, later, to treat the dam’s water budget as a fact of nature.

In 1997 Egypt launched the Toshka (New Valley) project, a plan to move Nile water out of the valley and into the Western Desert. Again, a major new use of a shared river. Again, no request for Ethiopia’s consent. The country that contributes most of the water was treated as a spectator.

Only when Ethiopia began to build on its own tributaries did “prior notification” become an urgent Egyptian principle.

## A rule that runs only one way is not a rule

Nobody serious argues that downstream countries have no interest in how a river is used. International water law speaks of equitable and reasonable utilisation, and of not causing significant harm. Those duties run in both directions. They do not give the first-mover a veto forever.

If notification is required before a new use, it was required in 1959, when Egypt and Sudan assigned themselves the river. It was required in 1960, when work began at Aswan. It was required in 1997, when Toshka was set in motion. A principle that applies only to Ethiopian concrete is not law. It is hierarchy.

## The law has already moved

On 13 October 2024 the Nile Basin Cooperative Framework Agreement entered into force. It is the basin’s attempt to replace colonial and bilateral carve-ups with a shared set of rules among the states that actually share the river. Egypt and Sudan have rejected it. Their rejection does not rewind the date.

The CFA will not, by itself, settle every dam, filling schedule or irrigation canal. What it does is end the pretence that 1959 is the last word, or that Ethiopia must ask permission to use water that rises on its own land, from a capital that never asked Ethiopia about Aswan.

Prior notification, applied fairly, would have started in Cairo. It never did. The record is the answer: Egypt never asked Ethiopia.

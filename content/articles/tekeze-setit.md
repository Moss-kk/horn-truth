Eritrea is often described as a Red Sea country, which is true, and as a country of rivers, which is much less true. The UN Food and Agriculture Organization puts the hydrological fact in a single sentence: there is only one perennial river in Eritrea, the Setit. Every other river is seasonal. It runs after the rains and then it fails.

That one year-round river does not rise in Eritrea. It is born in Ethiopia, where it is called the Tekeze.

## A highland river with two names

The Tekeze cuts a deep gorge through northern Ethiopia before it becomes a border river. Downstream, where it runs along the Ethiopian–Eritrean line and on toward Sudan, it is known as the Setit. Later, joined by the Atbara, it is one of the Nile’s eastern tributaries. About 13 percent of the water that reaches Aswan is traced to this system. The name changes. The source does not.

![The Tekeze/Setit flows from the Ethiopian highlands to the Eritrean border and on toward the Nile. Credits appear on the map.](/images/tekeze/tekeze_flow_map.jpg "The Tekeze/Setit flows from the Ethiopian highlands to the Eritrean border and on toward the Nile. Credits appear on the map.")

Look at the basin, not only the borderline. The great majority of the Tekeze–Setit catchment — more than 90 percent in standard basin accounts — lies inside Ethiopia. Eritrea receives the river after the highlands have already gathered the rain. That is why FAO water profiles also record a high external water dependency for Eritrea: in commonly cited AQUASTAT figures, more than half of the water the country uses, and in some presentations as much as 68.2 percent, originates beyond its borders.

This is not an insult. It is basin geography. A downstream state can have a legitimate interest in a shared river. It cannot rewrite the map so that the river appears to have been Eritrean all along.

## The gorge and the border

The photographs of the Tekeze gorge are easy to aestheticise, and they should not be. They show a river that has had to work for every kilometre, cut into Ethiopian rock, long before it is asked to serve farms, towns or a border post.

![The Tekeze gorge in northern Ethiopia. Photo: Jan Nyssen, CC BY-SA 4.0.](/images/tekeze/tekeze_gorge.jpg "The Tekeze gorge in northern Ethiopia. Photo: Jan Nyssen, CC BY-SA 4.0.")

Further north the same river becomes a political line. For a stretch it is the boundary itself. That is why Eritrea’s only perennial stream is also a shared stream. But a border river is not a river that begins at the border. The basin map is the honest picture: the catchment is overwhelmingly Ethiopian, the perennial flow is Ethiopian in origin, and the Eritrean reach is the last, driest chapter of a highland story.

![The Tekeze–Setit (Tekeze–Atbara) basin. Map: Mheberger, CC BY-SA 4.0.](/images/tekeze/tekeze_basin.jpg "The Tekeze–Setit (Tekeze–Atbara) basin. Map: Mheberger, CC BY-SA 4.0.")

## Why the fact is still argued

Water in the Horn is never only water. It is history, power and talking points. A country with one year-round river has a reason to speak carefully about that river. A country in which the river is born has a reason not to let the source be forgotten.

The FAO language is useful because it is dull. It does not belong to a ministry of foreign affairs. Eritrea has one perennial river, the Setit, and that river forms a border with Ethiopia. All the others are seasonal. If you follow the Setit upstream, you are in the Tekeze. If you follow the Tekeze upstream, you are in Ethiopia.

Any serious conversation about the Nile, about the Mereb-Gash, or about future water use in the northern Horn has to start there. Eritrea’s only year-round river is born in Ethiopia.

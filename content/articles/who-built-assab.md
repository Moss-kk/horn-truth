When people speak of Assab as if it had always been someone else’s harbour, they skip the expensive part of the story. The modern port and the oil refinery were twentieth-century Ethiopian projects. They were paid for by the Ethiopian state, built with Ethiopian labour, and run for the Ethiopian market. Eritrean independence in 1993 moved both assets to Asmara. Ethiopia received no compensation.

This is a draft. The figures below are taken from official Ethiopian, International Monetary Fund and World Bank material, and they should be read as a sourced record, not as a slogan.

## A port Ethiopia paid to build

Assab had been a Red Sea anchorage long before the 1950s. What Ethiopia built in the late imperial years was something else: a modern deep-water port meant to give a landlocked highland economy a door of its own, instead of depending only on Djibouti.

In 1958 the cornerstone of that port was laid, at a cost of more than 26 million Ethiopian dollars. The port opened in December 1961. Those dates matter. They belong to the federation and early union years, when Assab was developed as an Ethiopian national project, not as a gift from a future neighbour.

A year after the cornerstone, in 1959, the Soviet Union extended Ethiopia a loan of 400 million rubles. World Bank material on that credit records that it was used mainly for the Assab port and the Assab refinery. The point is not that Soviet engineers were present. The point is who contracted the debt, who would have to repay it, and whose trade the new facilities were designed to serve.

By the last years of the Derg, Assab was no longer a side door. A U.S. Library of Congress country study records that in 1988 the port handled about 71 percent of Ethiopia’s export-import trade. In fiscal year 1986/87 more than 2.8 million tons moved through it, including crude oil for the refinery.

## The refinery: Ethiopian workers, a Soviet crew, an Ethiopian company

The official history of the Ethiopian Petroleum Supply Enterprise is plain. The government planned a refinery at Assab in 1962. Construction began in January 1964 and finished in October 1967. About 500 specialists came from the Soviet Union. More than 3,000 professionals and labourers came from Ethiopia.

In 1967 the government organised the plant as a state institution, the Ethiopian Petroleum Refinery Share Association. It was not a private Eritrean company and it was not a Soviet gift sitting off the books. It was an Ethiopian state enterprise.

Its first capacity was 500,000 metric tons of crude a year. In 1971 that was raised to 650,000 tons to meet rising national demand. Some later accounts put the plant’s capacity in a range as high as 800,000 tons. Either way, this was Ethiopia’s only refinery — the place that turned imported crude into the fuel of a growing state.

## Handed over, then still paid for

In May 1991 the Eritrean liberation front took control of Assab, including the refinery. Ethiopia’s only oil port and only refinery were suddenly behind a new political line. Two years later, in May 1993, Eritrea became independent. The port and the refinery passed to Asmara. Ethiopia was not paid for the plants it had financed, built and staffed.

What followed is often forgotten. Ethiopia did not simply walk away from a facility it had lost on paper. It went on paying for the crude and the spare parts that kept the refinery running. The fuel came back to the Ethiopian market — but not all of it.

The IMF’s 1994 staff report on Eritrea is unambiguous. “The petroleum refinery at Assab produces petroleum products on behalf of both Eritrea and Ethiopia under mutual agreement.” In 1993 Eritrea purchased 169,871 metric tons of petroleum products from Ethiopia. That was 26.5 percent of production — about a quarter of the fuel — and about 90 percent of the quota then allocated to Eritrea.

So the picture in the first years of independence is this: an Ethiopian-built refinery, on a coast that had just been recognised as Eritrean, still running on crude Ethiopia paid for, with more than a quarter of the output going to Eritrea.

## Shutdown

The arrangement did not last. Official Ethiopian accounts describe growing maintenance costs, falling capacity and a plant that could no longer cover national demand. Ethiopia stopped using the Assab refinery in 1997 and turned more heavily to imported refined fuel. The plant went silent.

When the border war closed the port in 1998, visitors found a town whose economy had been built around Ethiopian cargo, Ethiopian fuel and Ethiopian labour. That was not an accident of geography. It was the afterlife of an Ethiopian investment.

The political argument over sovereignty will go on. The industrial record is harder to blur. Ethiopia laid out the money for the modern port in 1958, opened it in 1961, borrowed for port and refinery in 1959, and built the refinery between 1964 and 1967 with thousands of its own workers. In 1993 those assets were transferred without compensation. Until 1997 Ethiopia was still paying to keep the fuel flowing — including the share that went to Eritrea.

That is who built Assab.
